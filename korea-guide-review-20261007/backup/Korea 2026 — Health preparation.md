## 📅 Before you go

- [ ] **Book a pre-travel appointment.** Ideally four to eight weeks out. Tell them the route, the dates and the season — Chile to Korea, late October, six nights, one traveller avoiding meat.
- [ ] **Review vaccines** with your clinician. Routine boosters plus travel-specific cover depends on their assessment and your history. Ask specifically about anything due for typhoid or hepatitis A.
- [ ] **Get the Japanese encephalitis question answered**, not assumed. It is generally considered only for sustained rural or outdoor exposure, or long stays. Six days across three cities is usually below the threshold — **but your clinician decides, not this note.**
- [ ] **Prescriptions.** Carry them in original packaging with a copy of the prescription. Check whether your medication has a Korean import restriction. Note the generic name in Korean or in Latin script, not just the brand.
- [ ] **Assemble a small kit** — see below.
- [ ] **Record your allergies and medications on a card** you can show a clinician without speaking. Include blood type if you know it.

> [!warning] Prescription medicines are the item to check early
> Korea restricts some medicines, and pseudoephedrine — common in western cold remedies — is notably harder to get. If you rely on any specific medication, verify its import status **weeks ahead**. A pharmacy substitution on arrival is not guaranteed.

## ✈️ The long flight, both ways

Santiago to Seoul is roughly 24 hours door to door with a connection, and the return similar. Jet lag is the most likely thing to spoil this trip.

**Eastbound (Chile → Korea, −5 to −6 h)**
- Set your watch to Seoul time at the gate. Your body will be **16 hours ahead** of what it expects.
- **Sleep on the plane if you can** — the overnight hours of a westbound flight are the ones that map to your destination bedtime.
- Bright light on arrival, including the walk from Gimhae or the train. Avoid daylight naps on Day 1.
- Expect to wake around 03:00–05:00 on nights one and two. That is normal and it passes.

**Westbound home**
- Stay awake for the daylight portion and eat at local mealtimes.
- Set an alarm for morning light on arrival.

**The itinerary already accommodates this:** Day 1 is deliberately light and Day 2 is the first full day. Do not move things earlier.

> [!tip] Caffeine with a boundary
> Korea is a serious tea and coffee culture and it is everywhere. The easy mistake is drinking four cups and then being awake at 03:00. **One real coffee, then switch to citrus tea (유자차)** — which is what Korean cafés actually serve in the evening.

## 💊 A small travel kit

| Item | For |
| --- | --- |
| Paracetamol / ibuprofen | The standard headache and too-many-steps remedy |
| Oral rehydration salts | **The single most useful item for a food trip** |
| Antihistamine | Hay fever, or a reaction to something unfamiliar |
| Plasters and antiseptic | Gamcheon's steps are unforgiving |
| Imodium | Long-haul contingency |
| Rehydration | See above — buy in Korea, cheaper |
| Any personal prescription | See the warning above |

> [!info] What Korea gives you
> **Pharmacies are everywhere** — look for the green cross. Many are open late, and pharmacists will often handle minor complaints directly. Several sell foreign medication. **Prices are low**, so running out is not a crisis. Tap water is safe to drink in all three cities.

## 🍱 Food and what you avoid

Your brief: **no meat; fish is fine.**

> [!danger] The one sentence that handles most of it
> **간장 빼주세요** — *please leave out the soy sauce.* Korean soy sauce is very frequently made with **bonito or anchovy stock**, it is in nearly every stir-fry, stew and sauce, and it is not always disclosed. Saying this is understood instantly, even with no Korean. Asking only for "no meat" is not sufficient.
>
> For soups and stews — *jjigae*, *guk*, anything brothy — add **고기 육수 없이**, *no meat broth*. Stock is even less often volunteered in soup than in sauce.

**Lower-risk by nature:** temple vegetarian cooking (no meat ever enters it), plain rice, *sarangchae* banchan, fruit, bread, most café food, and seafood generally.

**Higher-risk:** anything with a brown sauce, stews of unknown base, and dried anchovy or bonito as a garnish — the garnish is often eaten.

> [!tip] One useful visual check
> Bonito flakes (*gaseo*, the small dried fish) look like dead fish on the dish and are routinely eaten as a garnish, not just seasoning. If one is sitting on your plate, leave it. The fish broth in *milmyu* is a different question — you said fish is fine, so that is your call rather than a problem.

## ☀️ Sun, cold and weather

| Exposure | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **UV** | Lower latitude than Chile in late October, but the light is **clean and low**, and it glares hard off stone, water and snow-free white walls. Sunglasses are not optional |
| **Cold** | 6–10°C nights. Wind on the DMZ coach and on Inwangsan makes it feel colder. Gloves, not just a jacket |
| **Rain** | October is the driest month — three to seven brief days. A compact umbrella |
| **Air quality** | Fine particulate is a variable in Seoul. Check **AirKorea** (에어코리아) daily. Sensitive lungs should have a mask |

> [!warning] Air quality is worth one app
> **AirKorea** is the official Korean air-quality service and reports PM2.5 hourly. Seoul in late autumn is usually acceptable, but it varies with weather and region. If you have asthma or any respiratory condition, check it before each outdoor block. Busan and Gyeongju are typically better than Seoul.

## 🏥 If you become unwell

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Emergency** | **112** — police, fire and ambulance, nationwide, 24 hours |
| **Tourist helpline** | **1330** — 24-hour, multilingual |
| **English-capable care** | Available at university hospitals and large general hospitals in all three cities |
| **Pharmacy** | Green cross. Common late-evening stock |

**What to do, in order:**
1. Something serious — chest pain, breathing difficulty, a serious fall, loss of consciousness — **call 112 immediately.** Say where you are and describe the problem. English works.
2. Moderate — call **1330** for a clinic recommendation and language support.
3. Minor — a pharmacy will usually resolve it, and pharmacists handle straightforward complaints.
4. **Have your passport and the clinic's name and address written down in Korean.** Shown at a counter it saves a great deal of confusion.

> [!danger] Know these two numbers before you land
> **112** and **1330**. Save them in your phone in Hangul as well as digits — **비상** means emergency. Screenshot both. There is no reason to look them up when it matters.

## 🚶 Wearable things

| Day | Risk |
| --- | --- |
| **Day 6, Gamcheon** | 3.5 m alleys, 300+ steps. The single highest-wear day |
| **Day 4, Wolji Pond** | Standing still in 5–9°C for ten minutes. Cold feet, not injury |
| **Day 5, Bulguksa / Seokguram** | Uneven stone terraces; the gravel at Seokguram |
| **Day 2, palaces** | Marble and flagstone, easy on feet, hard on knees |

> [!tip] Broken-in shoes, and a second pair if you own them
> The whole plan assumes 12,000–18,000 steps a day for six days. New shoes on Day 6 in a hill village is the one avoidable problem on this list. See [[Korea 2026 — Weather and Packing|the packing note]].
