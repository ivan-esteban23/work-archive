## A. Nutrition and daily functioning

Adequacy means meeting nutrient needs. Energy balance influences body weight over time; food quality, nutrient sufficiency and clinical health deserve separate attention. A diet can support weight loss yet be nutritionally poor, and a person can improve food quality without choosing weight loss.

Protein supplies amino acids for tissues and functional proteins; carbohydrate is an energy source; fats supply essential fatty acids and support cellular functions. Fiber supports bowel function and contributes to dietary quality. Legumes, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds offer different combinations of nutrients. WHO favors variety and unsaturated over saturated fats. Its reference for people older than ten includes at least 25 g/day naturally occurring fiber. These are educational foundations, not personalized macronutrient targets. [WHO, Healthy diet, January 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet).

Water comes from food and beverages. EFSA's adequate-intake values refer to total water, not additional plain water everyone must drink. Activity, heat, illness and prescribed fluid restrictions change the context. Keep water accessible rather than adopting a fixed challenge. [EFSA, Dietary Reference Values summary, 2017](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2017_09_DRVs_summary_report.pdf).

Hunger is not a precise test of nutritional adequacy. A controlled study in men with overweight/obesity found higher protein increased satiety, while more eating occasions did not reliably do so. This supports a possible meal-composition effect, not a universal high-protein prescription. [Leidy et al., 2010, appetite trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20339363/).

Breakfast evidence depends on the outcome. A review of adult cognition studies found a modest memory advantage but mixed attention/executive-function findings; it does not establish that everyone concentrates better after breakfast. Weight trials cannot answer that question by themselves. [Galioto and Spitznagel, 2016, breakfast and cognition review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27184286/).

## B. Diet-pattern comparison

| Pattern | Evidence and applicability | Adequacy and practical considerations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mediterranean style | Corrected PREDIMED supports cardiovascular benefit in older, high-risk Spanish adults; not proof it is uniquely best for every person | Flexible food pattern; cost depends on substitutions and access. Starting alcohol is unnecessary |
| Vegetarian/vegan | Long-term clinical evidence is substantially observational; food selection matters within each label | Vegan eating needs reliable B12 from fortified foods or supplements. Plan other nutrients instead of assuming plant-based guarantees adequacy |
| Lower carbohydrate | Cochrane found broadly similar weight change and several risk markers versus balanced-carbohydrate weight-reduction diets through two years | Amount and replacement foods vary. Preserve nutrient sources; adherence and medications matter |
| Intermittent fasting | Trial evidence differs by protocol; alternate-day fasting had a small additional weight effect in a 2025 synthesis | Eating-window restriction does not ensure adequate nutrition. Fit to schedule, hunger and health context matters |

Sources: [PREDIMED corrected paper, Estruch et al., 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389); [NIH ODS, Vitamin B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-Consumer/); [Cochrane, Naude et al., 2022](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013334_low-carbohydrate-diets-or-balanced-carbohydrate-diets-which-works-better-weight-loss-and-heart); [BMJ fasting review, 2025](https://www.bmj.com/content/389/bmj-2024-082007).

## C. Key evidence and its limits

| Study | Design/population/time | Finding and limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Estruch et al., corrected PREDIMED, 2018 | 7,447 older high-risk adults; trial with allocation deviations | Main adjusted hazard ratios: 0.69 olive oil, 0.72 nuts versus control. Both confidence intervals below 1. Sensitivity analyses must be identified separately; trial irregularities remain relevant |
| Naude et al., Cochrane, 2022 | 61 RCTs; 6,925 adults with overweight/obesity; up to two years; search June 2021 | Probably little/no difference in weight and selected risk markers. Similar energy prescriptions in most trials are not precisely measured matching of intake; no lifetime-mortality answer |
| Seidelmann et al., 2018 | ARIC plus cohort meta-analysis | U-shaped carbohydrate/mortality association and different plant/animal substitution associations. Self-report, residual confounding and reverse causation prevent deriving an ideal personal percentage |
| BMJ fasting network review, 2025 | 99 randomized trials; varied protocols and durations; search November 2024 | Alternate-day fasting versus continuous restriction: additional 1.29 kg loss, below authors' 2 kg minimally important threshold. Protocols are not interchangeable; no universal superiority |
| Liu et al., 2022 | 139 adults with obesity; 12-month RCT | Adding 08:00–16:00 timing to calorie restriction produced no significant additional weight effect; interval still permits benefit or no benefit. Not proof of the precise mechanism |
| Gu et al., 2020 | 20 healthy adults; acute crossover, 18:00/22:00 dinner and fixed sleep | Late dinner worsened acute glucose/fat-metabolism responses, without changing sleep architecture. Does not establish long-term weight gain or everyone's cutoff |

Direct original records: [PREDIMED](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29897866/), [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013334_low-carbohydrate-diets-or-balanced-carbohydrate-diets-which-works-better-weight-loss-and-heart), [Seidelmann](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30122560/), [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/389/bmj-2024-082007), [Liu](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35443107/), [Gu](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32525525/).

For vegan nutrition, NIH identifies higher B12-deficiency risk without reliable provision. Iron, calcium, iodine and other nutrient planning depend on actual foods and life stage; supplement amounts require individual context. Fasting or major restrictions need particular care with glucose-lowering medicines, pregnancy, low weight or an eating-disorder history. These considerations do not establish that any applies to you. [NIH B12 guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-Consumer/).

## D. Labels, timing and uncertainties

Free sugars include added sugars and sugars in honey, syrups and juice; they exclude sugars in intact fruit and milk. WHO recommends free sugars below 10% of energy, with possible additional benefit below 5%. At an illustrative 2,000 kcal, those thresholds correspond to 50 g and 25 g. Adult sodium guidance is below 2 g/day, approximately 5 g salt. These references are not your targets. [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet).

Check the label's units and basis: sodium or salt, per serving or per 100 g/ml. Calculate the amount eaten from that basis. For example, a hypothetical food with 400 mg sodium per 100 g supplies 600 mg in 150 g. This is arithmetic, not a real product assessment. “No added sugar” does not mean no sugar, and a processing category alone does not tell you every nutrient in a food.

Reflux illustrates why context matters: NIDDK says people with nighttime GERD symptoms may benefit from meals at least three hours before lying down. This is symptom-specific guidance, not a universal metabolism rule. Individual triggers vary; do not automatically remove every listed food. [NIDDK, GERD nutrition, reviewed July 2020](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/acid-reflux-ger-gerd-adults/eating-diet-nutrition).

No source here establishes a universal breakfast requirement, an exact best carbohydrate percentage, or a diet label that guarantees energy or concentration. Long-term head-to-head clinical trials and individualized meal-timing evidence remain limited. Null trial results are not proof that all approaches are identical.

## E. Five flexible possibilities

These are choices to consider, not a prescribed routine:

1. Add a convenient food source currently missing from meals, such as a legume or vegetable.
2. Include an accessible protein source in a meal that otherwise feels unsatisfying; observe rather than predict the effect.
3. Compare two similar products on the same label basis and choose what fits taste, cost and nutrient priorities.
4. Keep a practical fallback meal available for busy days. Frozen and canned foods can be useful ingredients.
5. Notice meal timing, hunger and comfort briefly before choosing a diet label or restrictive schedule.

## F. Five personalization questions

1. What matters most: energy, concentration, digestion, cooking consistency or another outcome?
2. What are your ordinary meal times and food choices, including workday/weekend differences?
3. What foods do you enjoy, avoid or need to exclude?
4. What budget, storage, cooking access and schedule constraints should the plan fit?
5. Are there relevant conditions, medicines, nutrient deficiencies or other circumstances that affect a proposed change?
